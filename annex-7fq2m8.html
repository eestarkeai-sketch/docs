1. Where in the band, and why

The band is not a negotiation range you slide down under pressure. It is a scoping range you place accurately. Two engagements in the same format can sit at opposite ends of the same band and both be correctly priced.

Move up when:

  • The room is more senior. Executive concentration is the single strongest upward factor.
  • The content is customized to their situation rather than delivered as it stands.
  • The host is a prestige organization.
  • Delivery is in-house and private to one company.
  • Exclusivity is requested.
  • Significant preparation is required before anyone enters the room.
  • Visibility is high, internally or externally.

Hold at base when:

  • It is a chamber relationship, where base pricing is the rule and the value is the introductions.
  • It is a first activation or a pilot.
  • Customization is minimal.
  • The recurring potential is strong and you are buying the second engagement with the first.
  • The operational burden is low.
  • It is a long-term partner.

One structural rule that overrides the sliders: in-house private work never starts below $12,500, whatever the relationship. A warm chamber connection does not buy a company a discount on private delivery.

2. The rule that protects your commission

Never discount the price. Adjust the scope.

This is canon, it is the most valuable selling skill you will learn here, and it is also the thing that decides what you earn. A discount takes money out of your own commission to solve a problem that had a better answer.

When a buyer says the number is high, you do not have a price problem. You have a scope problem or a value problem, and both have better answers than a smaller number.

The ladder, in order.

  1. Reframe to value. What is the decision they are stuck on actually costing them? Ask, do not assert. Let them do the arithmetic out loud.
  2. Reduce scope at the same price. Fewer participants, less customization, no follow-up session, no exclusivity, a shorter room. Same number, smaller thing. This is the move most sellers never make and it works more often than any other.
  3. Pilot at base with a written path to the full program. Your best move on a hesitant first buyer, and the mechanism by which a $12,500 session becomes a $37,500 series.
  4. Surface the recurring logic. One session is an expense. A rhythm is a capability. Buyers fund capabilities out of a different budget than they fund events.
  5. Hold the number.

Worked example. A company wants the Leadership-Team Working Session but says $22,000 is beyond this quarter. You do not offer $18,000. You offer the In-House Executive Session at $12,500 for the wider leadership group now, with the executive-team session scheduled for next quarter at $22,000, and you write both into the same proposal. You have protected the price, kept the relationship, and put a second engagement on the calendar. Your commission across the two is larger than the discount you did not give.

3. The three strategic exceptions, none of which are yours

These exist, they are documented, and you have no authority over any of them. If a conversation is heading toward one, bring Ethan in rather than hinting that it might be possible.

  1. Co-presenting with a high-profile speaker.
  2. Equity or advisory compensation, and only for a top-tier company.
  3. A multi-year chamber partnership volume arrangement, and only at tier two and above.

4. Books

Books are purchased separately at cost and that is stated on every proposal. The only exception is a package that explicitly includes them.

5. What this annex does not cover

Anything with no price in the record. The six-week certificate cohorts, licensed curriculum and facilitator licensing have no evidenced price anywhere in the estate, and coaching sits outside this practice entirely. Those are scoped by Ethan. Saying "that one is scoped rather than listed" is a complete answer.
